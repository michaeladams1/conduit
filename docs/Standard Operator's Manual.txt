                      Freezing Weather Effect on Pump Systems
During freezing conditions, strict precautions must be taken to prevent catastrophic pump failure and equipment
damage. Follow these guidelines whenever ambient temperatures are at or below 32°F (0°C).

Fluid Dynamics & Freezing
It is a dangerous misconception that flowing fluid will not freeze. At subzero temperatures, pumpage is subject
to freezing regardless of the following conditions:
     • Flow State: Whether the flow is steady (constant velocity) or unsteady (variable velocity).
     • Viscosity: While viscosity measures internal fluid friction, even highly viscous "thick" fluids can freeze
         and hamper flow.
     • Motion Type: Fluids will freeze regardless of whether the flow is rotational (turbulent/spinning),
         irrotational (non-spinning), or shear (uneven velocities in the same direction).

Draining the System (During Downtime)
To prevent ice expansion from cracking internal components, the system must be completely void of liquid
when not in use:
   • Pump Volute & Suction Line: Remove the drain plug or open the fittings (typically a ball valve) at the
       bottom of the volute to drain all fluid.
   • Priming Chamber: Ensure the chamber is fully drained. Residual fluid can freeze, rendering the float
       or linkage inoperable.
   • Vacuum Priming Systems: Units equipped with vacuum primers must have the diaphragm housing
       drained to prevent damage to the elastomer membrane.
   • Discharge Lines: Provide drainage for any "low spots" in the discharge piping where liquid may collect.
       If lines are disconnected for drainage, ensure they are properly re-plumbed before next use.

Pre-Start Inspections
   • Engine Exhaust: Verify that the engine exhaust rain cap opens and closes freely. Ice or debris
       obstruction can cause exhaust restriction, leading to rapid soot accumulation within the engine and/or
       enclosure.
   • Freeze Check: If fluid has already frozen inside the volute, DO NOT START THE ENGINE. Starting
       a frozen pump can shear shafts or destroy impellers.

Thawing & Preventative Heating
If the system is frozen, the volute must be thawed completely before operation.
     • Do not use high-temperature heat sources to thaw the pump. Rapid temperature changes can cause the
        volute to crack.
   •   Use a high-watt heat lamp placed under the volute and discharge check valve or apply controlled heat
       tracing with insulation.
   •   When using heat tape on pipes, apply at least two tapes (one on each side) and wrap with insulation
       secured by zip-ties. Always follow the heat tape manufacturer’s specific instructions.

                    Protect your investment: If the temperature drops, drain the pump!

Routine Operational Checks
Perform regular inspections to monitor the condition of the pump, coupling, stuffing box, and bearings.
Immediately investigate any of the following:
    • Noise: Any slight or sudden increase in operating volume.
    • Heat: Any unusual temperature rise in mechanical components.
Refer to the Troubleshooting section for diagnostic assistance.

Mechanical Seal Maintenance
The mechanical seal prevents air and abrasive particles from entering the stuffing box. It is designed for
prolonged dry-running but requires continuous lubrication.
    • The seal relies on a constant oil supply for cooling and lubrication. Never operate the pump without
       verifying that the seal oil reserve is full.
    • When servicing or replacing the seal, ensure the seal chamber remains entirely free of dirt and debris.

Bearing Lubrication
The pump-end bearings are grease-lubricated. Proper grease volume is critical to prevent overheating.
   •   Maintain the bearing housing at approximately one-third (1/3) full of high-quality ball bearing grease.
   •   Do not overfill the housing; excess grease will cause the bearings to run hot.
   •   Add a small amount of grease every 2 to 8 months. If necessary, remove old grease to ensure the
       housing does not exceed the 1/3 capacity limit.
   •   Use extreme care to prevent dirt or grit from contaminating the grease.

Wear Ring Clearance and Efficiency
The clearance between the suction cover and the impeller significantly impacts operating efficiency.
Replaceable wear rings are installed to maintain this gap.
   • Inspect wear rings regularly. Replace the rings when the clearance increases by approximately 0.020”
       (0.5 mm) beyond the factory specification.

                                 Eliminating Pump Problems
Understanding Cavitation
Cavitation is the formation and subsequent collapse of
vapor bubbles within a liquid. This occurs in areas of
relatively low pressure around an impeller. When
these bubbles implode, they trigger intense
shockwaves that cause significant erosion to the
impeller and pump housing.
Diagnostic Signs:
    • A distinct "popping" sound.
    • A noise like "pumping marbles" or gravel.
    • Excessive vibration




Suction Cavitation
Suction cavitation occurs when the pump is "starved" of fluid due to low pressure or high vacuum conditions at
the suction side.
 How it happens: Bubbles form at the "eye" of the impeller. As they move toward the discharge side, they are
 compressed back into liquid, causing them to implode against the impeller face.
 Damage Profile: The impeller will show pitting or
 missing material, eventually resembling a sponge.
 Common Causes
    • Clogged filters or strainers.
    • Blockage in the suction piping.
    • Operating the pump too far to the right on the
        pump curve.
    • Poor piping design or excessive suction lift.
    • Failure to meet Net Positive Suction Head
        (NPSH) requirements.

Discharge Cavitation
Discharge cavitation occurs when discharge pressure is extremely high, typically when the pump is operating at
less than 10% of its Best Efficiency Point (BEP).
How it happens: High pressure prevents fluid from exiting the pump, forcing it to recirculate internally. This
high-velocity flow between the impeller and housing creates a vacuum at the housing wall, leading to bubble
formation and implosion.
Damage Profile: Premature wear of impeller tips and the pump housing. In extreme cases, the resulting
shockwaves can break the impeller shaft.

Operating Procedures & Prevention
Start-up and Valve Control
To prevent shaft deflection and seal failure, follow these protocols:
   •   Ensure suction valves are fully open during operation. Never use a suction valve to throttle flow.
   •   Use a "soft start" protocol rather than jumping immediately to full capacity. This prevents the shaft from
       deflecting and contacting the stuffing box or casing.
   •   Avoid partially or completely closing the discharge valve for extended periods. While common for flow
       control, it can cause high radial loads, shaft deflection, increased heat, and severe vibration.
Efficiency and Wear Rings
   • Wear rings separate high- and low-pressure
       zones.
   • As operating hours accumulate, the clearance
       between the wear ring and impeller increases.
       This allows high-pressure liquid to leak into
       low-pressure areas, significantly reducing
       pump efficiency.
           o The figure to the right is an example of
               excessive wear.


Maintenance: Seals and Piping
Mechanical seals are precision components. The elastomers (boots and O-rings) and mating surfaces are easily
compromised.
   • Prevent contact with dirt, grease, or skin oils. Contaminants from handling can cause seal misalignment
      and premature failure.
   • Do not scratch or mar the parallel mating surfaces during installation.




       Damaged seal due to improper installation                        Seal with gas implosion pitting
Piping Design
   • Avoid inverted “U” fittings, which trap air.
   • Avoid sharp elbows immediately preceding the pump suction, as they produce turbulence that leads to
       cavitation.
   • In vertical pumps, the stuffing box is often at the highest point, which traps vapor. This vapor must be
       bled or vented to prevent the seal from running dry. Ensure the seal flush remains unobstructed and
       active.

3.1: Vacuum Pump
3.1.1: Redi-Prime® (RP) Automatic Priming System
The Redi-Prime® (RP) system is designed for high-speed, automatic priming with low maintenance
requirements.
System Operation
   • The belt-driven vacuum pump (A) draws air from the suction spool (231) and separator assembly (B)
      through the hose (421).
   • As air is removed, the water column rises until the pump is fully flooded.
   • The priming system remains active as long as the engine is running to ensure the pump stays primed
      during operation.
   • A discharge check valve (414) prevents the liquid from draining after shut-off, maintaining the prime for
      the next start-up.




Maintenance and Inspection
If water accidentally enters the vacuum pump, use the drainage petcock located at the bottom of the diaphragm
housing to remove the liquid.

                    9.1.1: Steps For Determining Correct Load Limit – Trailer

1. Understanding Ratings (GVWR and GAWR)
Every trailer is equipped with a Federal Certification/VIN Label, typically located on the forward half of the
road-side (left side) of the unit. This label provides two critical values:
    • Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR): The maximum allowable weight of the fully loaded trailer.
       This includes the weight of the trailer itself, the pump, all fluids, and any additional cargo.
    • Gross Axle Weight Rating (GAWR): The maximum weight a specific axle is designed to support. On
       multi-axle units, a rating is provided for each individual axle.
2. Cargo and Tire Placards
For trailers with a GVWR of 10,000 lbs or less, a Tire and Loading Information Placard is located near the VIN
label.
    • This placard states the Maximum Cargo Capacity as a single weight value.
    • Even if you have not reached the "Maximum Cargo" limit, the total weight of the unit must never
        exceed the stated GVWR.
3. Managing Onboard Fluids
When calculating your load, you must account for all onboard liquids. Unlike fixed equipment, fluids are
"disposable cargo" and their weight can be managed to stay within limits.
    • Fixed Weight: Propane containers (filled) are generally considered part of the trailer's base weight
       before cargo is added.
    • Variable Weight: Water and fuel are significant weight factors.
           o Example: A 100-gallon freshwater tank adds approximately 830 lbs to your load.

4. Load Distribution and Balance
How you place cargo is just as important as how much you carry.
    • Distribute weight evenly from side-to-side and front-to-back to prevent overloading a single tire or axle.
    • Place heavy items as low as possible and as close to the axle positions as reasonable.
    • The only way to confirm your actual weight is at a public scale. Consult your dealer for the best methods
       to capture weights for the hitch (tongue weight), individual axles, and the total unit.


                                WARNING: Under-Inflation and Overloading
It is the air pressure that enables a tire to support a load. Overloading or under-inflation causes abnormal tire
flexing, generating excessive heat. This heat can lead to sudden, catastrophic tire failure. Always verify your
PSI against the VIN label or Tire Placard—never exceed the maximum pressure stamped on the tire sidewall.

                9.1.2: Steps For Determining Correct Load Limit – Tow Vehicle

1. Locate the Placard
    • Find the statement on your vehicle’s tire and loading placard that reads: "The combined weight of
       occupants and cargo should never exceed XXX kg or XXX lbs."
2. Determine Occupant Weight
    • Identify the combined weight of the driver and all passengers who will be riding in the vehicle.
3. Calculate Remaining Capacity
    • Subtract the combined weight of the driver and passengers from the total weight (XXX) found in Step 1.
4. Identify Available Cargo Limit
The resulting figure is the Available Amount of Cargo and Luggage Capacity.
    • Example Calculation:
           o Placard Limit: 1,400 lbs
           o Occupant Weight: 5 passengers at 150 lbs each (750 lbs total)
           o Available Capacity: 1,400 lbs - 750 lbs = 650 lbs
5. Assess Cargo Load
    • Determine the combined weight of all luggage, tools, and cargo being loaded. This weight must not
       exceed the available capacity calculated in Step 4.
6. Account for Towing (Tongue Weight)
    • If you are towing a trailer, the weight of the trailer tongue is transferred to the tow vehicle. This "hitch
       weight" reduces the available cargo capacity calculated in Step 4. Consult your tow vehicle’s manual to
       determine the specific impact of this weight transfer.

                      9.2: Tire Maintenance and Safety (NHTSA Guidelines)

According to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), maintaining properly inflated and
high-quality tires is the most effective way to prevent tire failure, improve vehicle handling, and increase fuel
economy. The following is a brief overview of NHTSA’s tire safety standards. For more information, please visit
the NHSTA vehicle safety website.
Tire Pressure Management
   •   Check the pressure of all tires (including the spare) at least once a month and before any long-distance
       trip.
   •   For an accurate reading, always measure pressure when the tires are "cold"—meaning the vehicle has been
       stationary for at least three hours or has been driven less than one mile.
   •   Recommended PSI: Use the value found on the vehicle’s tire information placard or in the owner’s
       manual.
   •   Maximum PSI: Do not inflate tires to the pressure listed on the tire sidewall; that number is the maximum
       pressure the tire can hold, not the recommended operating pressure for the vehicle.
   •   Tire pressure drops in cold weather and rises in hot weather. Re-check pressure whenever there is a
       significant change in outside temperature.




Tread Depth and Inspection
Tread provides the traction necessary to prevent slipping, especially on wet or icy roads.
   • Tires are considered unsafe and must be replaced when the tread is worn down to 2/32 of an inch.
   • Most tires have built-in raised sections in the bottom of the tread grooves called wear bars. When the
       tread is flush with these bars, the tire must be replaced.
   • The Penny Test: Place a penny into a tread groove with Lincoln’s head upside down. If you can see the
       top of Lincoln’s head, the tread is too thin and the tire should be replaced.
   • Regularly check sidewalls and treads for:
           o Punctures, cuts, or scrapes.
           o Bulges or "bubbles" (indicating internal structural damage).
           o Cracks due to aging or sun exposure.




Premier Pump & Power, LLC                              40                         Operator’s Manual / March 2026
Tire Aging and Service Life
   •   The 6-Year Rule: Regardless of tread depth, many vehicle manufacturers recommend replacing tires
       every six years. Rubber degrades over time due to environmental factors, even if the vehicle is not driven
       frequently.
   •   Remember that spare tires age even when not in use. Inspect them for "dry rot" (cracking) annually.
Rotation, Alignment, and Balance
   •   Rotate tires every 5,000 to 8,000 miles (or as specified in the maintenance schedule) to ensure even wear
       and maximize tire life.
   •   If the vehicle "pulls" to one side or the steering wheel vibrates, have a professional check the wheel
       alignment. Improper alignment causes rapid, uneven tire wear.
   •   Every time a tire is replaced or moved on the rim, it must be professionally balanced to prevent vibration
       and premature wear on suspension components.




Load Limits and Overloading
   •   Overloading a vehicle creates excessive heat within the tire, which can lead to a sudden "blowout" or tread
       separation.
   •   Never exceed the Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR) or the Gross Axle Weight Rating (GAWR) listed
       on the vehicle's safety certification label.
